THE THINKING
01
The Insight

Travel in India has changed materially over the past decade. People are no longer ticking landmarks off a list. They are after the experience, the hidden place, the story that belongs to them alone. SideQuest is built for that traveller: the one who follows curiosity instead of an itinerary.

02
The Name

A side quest is the optional mission in a video game, the thing that is not the main objective. The term has since escaped gaming and entered everyday speech. The name arrives pre-loaded with meaning, so the work was not to invent a concept but to recognise one and avoid ruining it. It also describes the exact behaviour the brand wants to provoke.

03
The Voice

Gen Z vernacular held together by professional confidence. The mischievous travel companion who backs your questionable decisions and still makes sure you get home in one piece. The mischief is what makes it fun. The care is what makes it a brand you can trust with money.

04
The Structural Bet

Sell by terrain, not by demographic. Mountain, Beach, Forest, Desert. This is not a styling choice, it is an operating decision. It changes merchandising, navigation, and how the catalogue is built. A travel-fashion brand should be organised around where the customer is going, because that is the only question they are actually asking.

05
Where It Goes Next

Three extensions the system is already built to carry. Paired sets for couples, since a large share of Indian travel happens under the banner of a honeymoon and the set format extends naturally to two people. Content that stays user-centric rather than product-led, trading polished product shots for travel stories, underrated destinations, and practical tips. And the sharpest of the three: a movement built on tagging locations Instagram does not recognise. Real places with no pin. The audience does the work, the algorithm becomes the antagonist, and the brand line proves itself in public rather than merely claiming to be true.

Mission: break the itinerary. Vision: a generation that treats travel as spontaneously as a trip to the mall.

THE THINKING
01
The Brief

Great India Talent School is a free school in rural Tripura, run under NIVE, providing education to tribal and rural children who would otherwise have none. This was a fundraising campaign to keep it running — covering supplies, sponsorships, and full institutional adoption at three levels of giving.

02
The Photography Decision

Stock photography was never considered. For a cause this specific — children this real, in a situation this particular — generic imagery would have killed the emotional truth of the campaign before a single rupee was asked for. Real photos were requested directly from the school. They came through. A boy studying by window light. Girls walking to school through what looked like a little jungle. Kids in uniform, laughing. That's not something you can license.

03
The Design Approach

The campaign copy was dense by necessity — each creative had to carry a specific ask, a rupee amount, bank details, a QR code, and an 80G tax exemption notice. There wasn't room for emotional headlines doing heavy lifting. So the design deliberately stepped back and let the photography lead. Soft layout, clean type, no aggressive colour. The images do the feeling. The text handles the transaction.

04
The Colour System

Three donation tiers, three distinct colours — not arbitrary. Warm brown for supplies: accessible, warm, low-barrier. Red for child sponsorships: urgency, direct human impact. Blue for adopting the school: institutional weight, trust, long-term commitment. A donor could scan the feed and self-select their giving level before reading a word of body copy.

11 creatives across 3 tiers. Real photos from the school. Every rupee amount, every ask, earned by the image it sits next to.

Essay · Design Thinking

Everything is Designed.
Nothing is Just Decoration.

Ashish Rajpali  ·  2025

Ask someone what a designer does. They'll say something about colours. Maybe fonts. Maybe logos if they're feeling generous.

Nobody says door handles.

Nobody says the way a hospital corridor is lit so you feel less like you're about to receive bad news. Nobody says the signage that moves thousands of people through an airport without a single person stopping to think about where they're going. Nobody says the chair that held your back correctly for eight hours without you once noticing you were sitting.

Good design does that. It works so well you forget it's there. And the moment you forget it's there — that's when people stop calling it design.

The Fidget Spinner

I have a fidget spinner on my desk. These days every product is selling you two things at once — buy our fridge that also streams Spotify, this phone that also snaps into a projector, this laptop that's also a tablet that's also a drawing pad. Everything has a second function, a bonus feature, a reason to justify its existence. And then there's this. No screen. No second use. It spins. That's it. That's all. And it took the entire world by storm.

I like it because I actually do have trouble sitting idle. My hands keep spinning it, playing with it — until at some point I've completely forgotten I even own the thing and I'm just working. That's the whole product. Complete commitment to one purpose. And it delivered so completely that it disappeared into the background of my focus.

That's design. Not decoration.

The Invisible Infrastructure

Somewhere, a designer sat down and thought about how a city should feel to walk through at night. Which streets should be wide and which ones intimate. Where the light falls and where the shadow sits. How a park bench should be angled so strangers can sit near each other without feeling like they have to talk. These weren't decorative choices. They were decisions — real ones, with real outcomes — about how millions of people would move through their lives every single day. You feel those decisions every time you step outside. You just don't call them design.

The Finisher Problem

When people mix the two up, designers get treated like finishers. You make the real decisions, then hand it to the designer to make it pretty. Sprinkle some colour. Clean it up. Nice.

But the thinking was always the job. Design is every decision that shapes a human experience — and yes, that includes how something looks. Because how something looks is also a decision. Aesthetics aren't separate from function, they're part of it. Making something feel warm, or urgent, or trustworthy — that's not decoration, that's design doing its job at the surface level. It all belongs to the same discipline. Designers think about everything. The colour is just the part everyone notices.

The spinner on my desk does one thing perfectly. The hospital corridor asks nothing of you at the worst moment of your day. The bench lets two strangers share space without having to negotiate it.

None of that is pretty. All of it is designed.

Essay · Experience

The Cost of
the Wrong Room

Ashish Rajpali · 2026

Nobody told me the room I wasn't in would define my work more than the room I sat in every day.

I spent three years as the only designer in an organisation that didn't know it needed more than a designer. Every brief that reached my desk was already broken — not because anyone was careless, but because the conversation that should have happened before the brief never did. Strategies were decided in meetings I wasn't invited to. Mental images were formed. Assumptions were made. And then a request landed on my desk: "Make a poster. 10 ads. Design an invitation."

No why. No who. No what are we actually trying to say.

One time I was asked to design a poster for a student exchange program. No brief, no direction — just an itinerary. I made the best call I could and picked the most recognisable locations. Upon review I was given the look. "You missed the important ones." The important ones nobody mentioned. The ones decided in a meeting I was never part of.

That look is what this article is about.

It's not the look of a bad designer being corrected. It's the look of a process that was broken from the start — and a designer standing at the end of it absorbing blame that was never theirs to carry.

When there's no Art Director in the room, no one asking why before asking what — the designer becomes a production machine. Fast hands, no seat at the table. And slowly, without noticing, you start to shrink. You stop asking questions because the room laughs when you do. You stop pushing because nothing moves. You start measuring your worth in how quickly you can turn a request around instead of how well you can think through a problem.

That's the cost of the wrong room. Not just frustration — erosion.

I'm soo done eroding.

A CD's Diagnosis

Air India
Rebrand

Brand: Air India (Tata Sons)  ·  Agency: FutureBrand London/Mumbai  ·  Year: 2022–23

Tata acquired Air India in 2022 — inheriting decades of debt, broken operations, and a reputation that had become a national punchline.

The brief tried to do four things at once: distance from government-era baggage, signal global premium, retain Indian identity, absorb Vistara. That's not a design brief. That's too many problems in one room, and design can't fix all of them.

The Maharaja

He wasn't just famous — he was trusted. Globally recognised, impossible to replicate, decades of genuine affection. And they quietly shelved him.

The story was right there. An airline founded by Tata, nationalised against his wishes, returned to the family 70 years later. The Maharaja, finally free. That's a campaign. Nobody built it.

The Vista Mark

I'm Indian, grew up around Jharoka architecture, and I only got the reference after I read about it online. A symbol that needs explanation hasn't done its job.

The concept wasn't wrong — they were too nervous about being "too Indian" for a global audience. That nervousness is visible in the final mark.

Removing Devanagari

Script is not decoration. It tells you who the design was made for.

FutureBrand London led this project, and it showed. When the people in the room aren't from the culture, decisions like this slip through.

Aubergine

Red had 91 years behind it. Aubergine came in because Vistara was being absorbed — a logistics call dressed up as a creative one. The palette felt like a committee made it. Because one did.

The Actual Failure

The identity launched August 2023. Fleet refurbishment started mid-2024. Passengers got a premium brand promise on aircraft with old interiors and unresolved complaints.

That's not a branding failure. That's a trust failure.

No campaign. No conversation with the public about what had changed or why. The identity just dropped — and in the silence, people filled the gap with every frustration they already had.

Where This Lands

India's hospitality, art, and cultural texture is something no other airline can claim. That was Air India's one differentiator. The thing Emirates and Singapore Airlines could never buy.

They spent the rebrand softening it.

Fix operations first. Let the experience speak. Build the identity around what's actually true — not what you're hoping to become. Then tell people. In that order.

An identity can confirm a transformation. It cannot create one.

Observation · Narrative Design · Anime

One Piece & The
Architecture of
Revelation

Eiichiro Oda · 1997–Present  ·  Personal Essay  ·  2024  ·  3 min read

One Piece doesn't tell you what it is — it just keeps getting bigger until you understand it yourself.

A personal observation on how Oda built the most real world inside the most unreal one.

How It Starts

When I first watched Alabasta, I thought: great villain, sad village, evil ruler. Good story. It felt like another anime doing what anime does. I had no idea I was already inside something enormous.

It wasn't until Skypiea — Roger's Poneglyph, the realisation that he was here centuries ago — that the scale cracked open. But even then the emotions didn't all arrive at once. They unfolded arc by arc. Accumulated.

"This isn't just about some treasure." — Ashish, somewhere around Skypiea
Worth noting
The retrospective assembly of meaning — Oda gave you a map you could only read backwards. That's not just good storytelling. That's how memory works. You understand the shape of your own life the same way.

One Piece doesn't give you the manual — you just keep going until one day you look back and realise you were already inside something huge. But that's the thing: neither does life. Nobody sits you down at 15 and explains what's actually happening around you. You live through things — relationships, failures, moments that feel small — and only later, with distance, do you understand what they were part of. The meaning is always assembled in retrospect. One Piece just makes that process visible.

A Cartoon World with the Realest Problems

The world is maximally absurd on the surface — a rubber man, islands in the sky, a clown who detaches his own body parts in a fight. Oda makes the vessel as ridiculous as possible. And then underneath: slavery, colonialism, corrupt institutions protecting themselves over people. The same human tendency to control and seize, reappearing on every island, across every arc.

Give humans every crazy possibility in the world and they'll still build the same hierarchies and crush the same people. The absurdity is the Trojan horse. The real world problems are what was inside it the whole time.

Worth noting
The vessel is absurd so the cargo can be real. Every writer who ever used genre fiction to say true things knows exactly what Oda was doing. The comedy is load-bearing structure.
What Luffy Actually Represents

Luffy is the embodiment of how it should be in a world full of chaos. He simply ignores the broken systems and moves anyway. It looks reckless — but everywhere he goes, things get more free, not more chaotic. His freedom isn't the absence of problems. It's the refusal to let control become the answer to them.

What Oda Is Actually Doing to Children

A kid watching One Piece at 10 doesn't understand colonialism — but they understand what the World Government did to Ohara. They don't understand institutional corruption — but they understand Spandam. And when they grow up and the news becomes real, they won't be phased by it.

"They're watching the news, but not really." — Ashish

Oda has been inoculating an entire generation against the real world through the back door — for 27 years, inside a story about a boy who wants to be King of the Pirates. That's not entertainment. That's moral education disguised as a cartoon.

One Piece doesn't give you the world. It gives you a way of seeing it — and by the time you realise that, you've already been changed.

The Last of Us
Part II — A Game
That Made Me Play
the Villain

Naughty Dog  ·  PS4  ·  July 2020  ·  Personal thoughts, written in the moment

TLoU2 goes dark, fast. Joel — the guy you spent an entire game protecting and rooting for — is brutally killed within the first hour. The character responsible is Abby, and naturally, I hated her. I set out on Ellie's path burning with that rage.

The Story
"Dude, I literally set out to kill this bitch and now you're making me play as her?!" — Ashish, halfway through the game

Then the game pulls the rug: halfway through, you're suddenly playing as Abby. I was appalled. But within an hour, I understood her. I sympathised. The story doesn't justify what she did — it just shows you why. That shift in perspective is what makes this game genuinely uncomfortable in the best way.

Worth noting
The story doesn't justify what she did — it just shows you why. That's one of the sharpest lines in any piece of media criticism I've read. Empathy and forgiveness are not the same thing. Most storytelling conflates them. This game doesn't.
What the Gameplay Does Differently

The core mechanics don't change much between characters, but the feel does. Ellie is weaker but scrappy — resourceful, improvising. Abby hits harder but isn't as sharp with crafting. It's a small design choice that actually mirrors who they are as people.

And no matter who you're playing, the game never lets you off the hook. Every action has weight. It's unforgiving in a way that feels intentional — like the game itself is quietly judging your bloodlust.

Ellie's Arc

The flashbacks between Ellie and Joel are the emotional gut-punch of the whole game. You watch their bond grow, and then fracture when Ellie learns the truth about the Fireflies. My read: her revenge isn't just grief — it's guilt. She pushed Joel away, and never got the chance to fix it.

Abby's Arc — and a Detail Most People Miss

Her relationship with her father reframes the ending of the first game entirely. Her tangled feelings around Owen and Nora make her feel genuinely human. And then there's Lev — her decision to protect him and Yara is what finally moved her from antagonist to just another survivor trying to survive in a world that doesn't reward mercy.

One thing that stuck with me: Abby is afraid of heights. The game doesn't just tell you this — it shows you through the camera. When she's forced to look down from somewhere high, the angle slowly tilts and the frame wobbles slightly, as if the camera itself is struggling to hold steady. You don't just know she's afraid. You briefly feel the vertigo with her.

On this observation
Most criticism tells you what a game is about. This observation tells you what it does. Naughty Dog used the camera — a purely mechanical tool — as a psychological instrument. The frame doesn't observe her fear; it performs it. You don't watch Abby look down. You briefly inhabit her vertigo. That's the gap between craft and formula — and catching it while you're emotionally inside a revenge story is a director's eye at work.

The game forces you to be the villain — and then, slowly, makes you understand that the villain was always someone else's protagonist. That's not a comfortable thing to sit with. That's exactly the point.
